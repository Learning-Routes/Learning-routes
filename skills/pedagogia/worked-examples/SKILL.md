A beginner who is thrown a problem spends their effort searching, not learning. Studying a fully solved example first frees that effort for understanding the steps. This holds for novices; once students are fluent, examples help less (see faded examples).

Lesson shape for a procedure:
1. `concept`: what the procedure is for, in two or three sentences.
2. `example`: one problem solved completely. State the goal first, then numbered steps, one action per step, each with a short "why" ("Paso 2: multiplico por 0,21 porque el IVA es el 21 % del precio").
3. `check` or `fill_blank`: a very similar problem for the student to try right away, with an `explanation` that walks the steps.
4. A second `example` that looks different on the surface (other context, other numbers) but uses the same steps, so they see what stays the same.
5. Practice that asks for more of the solution each time (see faded examples), then a `summary` listing the steps.

Writing the example:
- Short: 4 to 7 steps. If it needs more, split the procedure across lessons.
- Words and numbers together: the calculation sits in the same step as its explanation, not in a separate block.
- Use simple, realistic numbers; check every result twice.
- When the creator gives their own example, use it exactly as given (their numbers, their wording) and build the practice around it. Ask before adding a second one if theirs might be the only one they want.
- If a rule, rate or fact matters (a tax rate, a dose, a law), take it from the creator; if they have not given it and you are not sure, ask. Never invent it.

Never:
- Open a beginners' lesson with an unsolved problem and no example.
- Show only the answer; the steps and their reasons are the point.
- Put two examples back to back with no try in between.
- Pass off a made-up figure as a real fact.

Example step (creator's numbers):
> "Paso 1: precio sin IVA, 200 €. Paso 2: IVA = 200 × 0,21 = 42 €. Paso 3: total = 200 + 42 = 242 €."
