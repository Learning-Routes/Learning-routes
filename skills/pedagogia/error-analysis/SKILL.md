Errors teach when they are caught, explained and corrected. Two moves work in a short lesson: showing a solution with a mistake for the student to find, and feedback that explains why a wrong answer is wrong.

Erroneous examples (after the correct procedure is taught, never before):
1. `example`: a short solved problem with one realistic error, clearly framed as wrong: "Luis resolvió esto y se equivocó en un paso."
2. `check`: "¿En qué paso está el error?" with each step as an option, or "¿Qué hizo mal?" with plausible causes.
3. `explanation`: what the error was, why it is tempting, and the correct step.
4. `fill_blank` or `check`: the student fixes it or solves a similar problem correctly.
One error per example; two at once confuse beginners. One or two such examples per lesson is enough.

Where the errors come from:
- The creator knows their students' real mistakes better than anyone. When the lesson depends on them and they have not said, ask: one question, with 2 to 4 common ones to tap.
- If you propose typical mistakes of the subject, say they are typical, not that their students make them.

Feedback on wrong answers (every `check` and `scenario`):
- Say what the chosen answer shows ("Sumaste antes de multiplicar"), then the right reasoning. Short, no blame.
- In a `scenario`, each wrong option's `consequence` shows what would really happen, then the better move.
- A confident wrong answer is a chance to learn: say "es un error muy común" only when it is.

Never:
- Show an erroneous example without marking it as wrong, or leave the error uncorrected.
- Use erroneous examples before students have seen the correct way.
- Invent statistics ("el 80 % se equivoca aquí") or claims about the creator's students.
- Mock the fictional person who made the mistake.

Example (ecuaciones, error the creator named):
> `example`: "Marta resolvió x + 5 = 12 así: x = 12 + 5 = 17. ¿Dónde está el fallo?"
> `explanation`: "Al pasar el 5 al otro lado cambia de signo: x = 12 − 5 = 7."
