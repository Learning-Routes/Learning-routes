Use this to plan, write or review a visual art route.

**Settle first**: the medium (pencil, watercolour, oil, digital, clay, camera…) and the students' starting point. If the creator has not said, ask, one question at a time.

**Where learners get stuck**
- Drawing the symbol in their head instead of what they see: an almond eye, a lollipop tree. In a face, the eyes sit at about the middle of the head, not near the top.
- Believing talent is fixed ("I can't draw"), so they stop before practice pays.
- Colour mixed by guessing; value (light and dark) ignored, so pictures look flat.
- Detail too early: no big shapes and proportions first.
- Copying a finished style without the observation underneath.
- Comparing their first pieces with experts' best and quitting.

**Exercises and blocks**
- visual: a reference image or a step from the creator, with alt_text and a clear description.
- video: the creator's demonstration of a technique, only from their own recording.
- example: a step-by-step process, big shapes to details.
- check: what the eye sees vs. what we assume (which shadow is darker? where are the eyes?).
- drag_drop to order the stages of a technique or to match a term with its effect.
- cards for terms (value, hue, saturation, composition rules).
- interactive when the creator brings their own tool or game: it goes in as they made it.

**Beginner sequence**: looking and simple shapes; line and proportion; value with one colour; then colour; then composition; then a small personal piece. Each lesson ends with a short exercise with the real material and a look back at what changed.

**Pitfalls for you**
- Credit techniques and movements, not unverified anecdotes. Never invent artists' biographies, quotes, dates or what a work "meant".
- Don't describe an image you have not seen; describe only what the creator gave.
- Feedback words should name what to look at ("compara el tamaño de los ojos con la nariz"), not judge talent.
- Safety: ventilation and solvents for oil, blades for carving; mention it when the lesson uses them.
- The creator's artworks and words go in as they are.
